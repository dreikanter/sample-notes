# Blog post draft — the cost of context switching

**Working title:** "Context switching is not your problem — transition time is"

**Audience:** developers and knowledge workers who work in distracted environments

---

The standard advice about context switching focuses on the wrong thing. We're told that switching between tasks destroys productivity, cite Gloria Mark's "23 minutes to regain focus" figure, and conclude: do fewer things, protect your blocks, say no.

This is correct but incomplete. The real cost isn't the switch itself — it's the time between putting one thing down and picking the next thing up. That gap is where productivity dies.

Watch what happens when you finish a meeting: ten minutes of Slack catching up, three minutes checking email, a minute or two of low-stakes browsing, and only then, slowly, the attempt to re-engage with actual work. By the time you've genuinely restarted, you've lost twenty minutes that felt like five.

The gap exists because transitions are cognitively uncomfortable. Ending one task creates mild cognitive dissonance — you're no longer in the flow state, you have an open loop, and the path back into concentration is unclear. The low-stakes browsing is a self-soothing mechanism.

**What actually helps:**

- End each work block with a brief written note on exactly where you are and what the next action is. This closes the loop before you leave it.
- Physical transition ritual (walk, coffee, etc.) that is intentionally bounded — five minutes, not twenty.
- The next block's first task should be the simplest possible thing that moves the work forward. Don't start a block by deciding what to work on.
