# Reading — "Pedro Páramo" by Juan Rulfo

Read in one sitting on a Sunday afternoon — it's about 130 pages in the Grove Press edition.

Juan Juan Rulfo published this in 1955. García Márquez said he could recite it from memory and that it opened the door to Latin American magical realism as a literary mode. Having now read it, the claim is entirely believable.

The novel opens with the narrator, Juan Preciado, traveling to the town of Comala to find his father, Pedro Páramo. The journey is strange from the beginning — he encounters people on the road who seem to belong to another time. By the time he arrives, it becomes clear that everyone in Comala is dead, and has been for years. The entire town is populated by ghosts, and Juan Preciado is one of them.

Rulfo narrates this with no explanation and no adjustment to the reader's confusion. The prose is spare, almost lyrical — very short chapters, each one a shard. Chronology is scrambled. Pedro Páramo's history (the title character, a cacique of brutal power) assembles itself out of fragments, voices, and fragments of dialogue.

**What makes it work:**

The matter-of-fact treatment of the dead. The ghosts don't know they're dead, or some do and some don't. Death is just a different mode of existence, not a rupture. This is presented not as surrealism but as the normal texture of the world.

The portrait of Pedro Páramo himself — a man who loved once and destroyed everything when he lost that love — is brutal and, eventually, oddly moving.
