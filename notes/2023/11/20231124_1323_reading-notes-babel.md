# Reading notes — Babel by R.F. Kuang

Finished on the train back. A few things I want to hold onto.

The central conceit — that translation always loses something, and that loss is power, and that colonialism is fundamentally an act of translation (taking from one context and forcing into another) — is not subtle, but it doesn't need to be. The book announces its thesis and then earns it through accumulation.

The silver-working system: the magic comes from the gap between a word and its translation. Engrave the word and its foreign equivalent on silver, and the space between the two meanings releases energy. This is clever because it literalizes what linguists actually argue: that translation is never equivalence, only approximation. Sapir-Whorf as magic system.

The most affecting section is the chapter in which Robin translates a letter from his father — technically accurate, emotionally falsifying — and understands something about what he's been trained to do.

Criticisms: the pacing in the middle section sags. The political arguments, while correct, are sometimes delivered as lectures rather than dramatized. A few of the secondary characters (particularly Ramy) are better as ideas than as people.

But the ending works because Kuang commits completely. No compromises, no redemption for institutions. The book is angry and earns its anger.

Recommended background reading for the historical period: [The British Library's online resources on the East India Company](https://www.bl.uk/the-east-india-company) provide useful context for the Oxford setting and the colonial machinery the novel critiques.
