# Reading notes — Piranesi by Susanna Clarke

Read in two sittings over the holiday break. Short enough that stopping felt wrong.

The form is everything here: a journal written by someone who doesn't know what he doesn't know, narrated with calm certainty about a world that the reader begins to understand is not what it seems. The dramatic irony is gentle rather than cruel — you feel protective of the narrator rather than superior to him.

Clarke's House — infinite corridors, statues, tidal halls — is one of the most fully realized imaginary spaces I've encountered. It's detailed enough to feel real and strange enough to resist reduction to allegory. The statues feel significant but never decoded into neat meaning; they remain part of the texture of the place.

The mystery plot resolves cleanly but doesn't overwhelm the book's better qualities. The best moment is not the revelation but the passage where Piranesi articulates his love for the House, which arrives before any revelation that would require him to revise it. The reader knows the revision is coming; his love remains unironic.

I came to this from *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell*, which I read years ago and found too dense to finish. *Piranesi* is the opposite: spare, precise, accessible. They share a willingness to commit fully to their premises without domesticating them.

Recommended: to anyone who has enjoyed Borges and found him too cold. [Clarke's author page at Bloomsbury](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/author/susanna-clarke/) for publication information.

Filed under: books I'll think about for longer than I expected.
