# Reflection on what expertise actually feels like

Had a conversation today with a colleague who's two years into the field and found myself genuinely uncertain how to describe what changes as you get more experience. The honest answer is: I mostly stopped having to think about certain things, and that freed up attention, but it also made me worse at explaining those things because they've become automatic.

This is the "curse of knowledge" problem in practice. I remember the exact moment when TCP handshakes stopped being something I had to consciously recall and became just background context. I can't teach from that moment anymore; I have to reconstruct what it was like not to know.

What I told my colleague: expertise is mostly about having better filters for what's worth worrying about. The junior engineer worries about everything because they don't know what matters. The experienced engineer has seen enough failures to have a rough probability map of what goes wrong and when.

But that map is also the danger — you stop expecting failure modes you haven't seen, which is exactly when novel failure modes appear. The most experienced people in a room are sometimes the ones most confidently wrong about an entirely new situation.

Dreyfus model of skill acquisition is the standard framework here. The stages from novice to expert roughly track with this: you start with rules and end with intuition, but intuition without reflective awareness is brittle. [Dreyfus & Dreyfus original paper](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/citations/ADA084551) is worth reading — it's from 1980 but the core observation hasn't aged.
