# The Dispossessed — reading notes

Finished Le Guin's novel last night after about two weeks of slow reading. It's one of those books I'd been circling for years without actually picking up, partly because "utopia fiction" can feel airless. This doesn't.

The structure — alternating chapters between Anarres and Urras — does something clever: neither world gets to be the uncomplicated good place. Anarres has the anarchist collective society that has calcified into social conformism enforced without any formal enforcement. Urras has abundance, beauty, and profound injustice. The reader ends up holding both in mind simultaneously, which seems like the point.

Shevek as a protagonist is unusual in that his inner life is dominated by physics rather than relationships. The sections on sequency and simultaneity in time theory could have felt like a cheat — sciency-sounding nonsense — but Le Guin makes it feel genuinely thought-through, and it rhymes with the book's structure in a satisfying way.

What stayed with me most is how she handles the absence of possessive language on Anarres. Small detail, but it does actual work — you notice its presence in the Urras chapters immediately once you've been immersed in its absence.

This sits alongside The Left Hand of Darkness and The Word for World is Forest in what I'd call her anthropological mode: fiction as a thought experiment with full emotional weight.

Next: probably Ursula's essay collection The Wave in the Mind, which I've had on the shelf even longer.
