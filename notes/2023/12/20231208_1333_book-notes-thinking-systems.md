# Book notes — Thinking in Systems

Donella Meadows' primer is one of those books that reorganizes categories you thought you understood. I came back to it after the sprint planning conversation about alert fatigue (see [[20231126_1325]]) because alert systems are a perfect example of a feedback loop with delays.

## Key concepts I'm still processing

**Stocks and flows:** Most things we care about are stocks — amounts that accumulate over time. Flows change stocks. The interesting insight is that you can't change a stock instantly — you can only change the rate of change. This seems obvious until you apply it to, for example, "fixing" organizational culture.

**Feedback loops:** Reinforcing loops amplify (growth or collapse). Balancing loops regulate (thermostats, population dynamics, buffer stock replenishment). The confusion between them is at the root of most counterintuitive policy outcomes — you try to strengthen a balancing loop and accidentally create a reinforcing one.

**Delays:** This is where systems become genuinely dangerous. A delay between an action and its effect causes oscillation if the actor doesn't know to wait. Overshoot. The shower example (turn cold because hot water is delayed; water becomes scalding; turn cold again) is the clearest demonstration of something that shows up in supply chains, antibiotic prescribing, and budget cycles.

**Leverage points:** Meadows' ranking of places to intervene in a system is worth memorizing. Parameters are the weakest intervention (changing the numbers). The most powerful: changing the goals of the system, changing the paradigm from which the system arises.

Source: [Chelsea Green Publishing edition](https://www.chelseagreen.com/product/thinking-in-systems/), edited posthumously by Diana Wright.
