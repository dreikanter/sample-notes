# Reading notes: "Debt — the first 5000 years" ch. 1–3

David Graeber opens by recounting a dinner party conversation where an economist insists that before money there was barter, and Graeber just... cannot find evidence for this in the historical record. He spends chapters two and three combing through anthropological literature and finding something stranger: credit systems that predate coinage by millennia, IOUs scratched into clay tablets in ancient Mesopotamia, elaborate systems of mutual obligation in small-scale societies that anthropologists observed directly.

The myth of barter is, Graeber argues, a thought experiment that economists inherited from Adam Smith and repeated as fact. Smith himself admitted he had no evidence for barter economies. What actually existed in pre-monetary societies was not "I'll trade my fish for your shoes" but layered networks of gifts, debts, and communal sharing, each with different moral weightings.

The distinction Graeber draws between debt and obligation is interesting. Obligations are qualitative and ongoing — the kind of thing you feel toward a neighbor who helps you move. Debt is a quantification that makes it possible to say the obligation is discharged. Money didn't eliminate debt; it gave debt a number.
