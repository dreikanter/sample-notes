# Short review: The Dispossessed

Ursula K. Le Guin's 1974 novel follows the physicist Shevek between two worlds: Anarres, a resource-scarce anarchist moon, and Urras, its capitalist, lush parent planet. The structure alternates chapters between the two timelines, converging at the end.

What the book gets right is the texture of both societies. Anarres is not a utopia in any comfortable sense — it has scarcity, social conformity enforced by peer pressure rather than law, and a language (Pravic) deliberately designed to suppress possessiveness. The Urrasti chapters are recognisably ours: opulent, stratified, with an underclass Le Guin makes sure we see.

Shevek is a physicist working on a Unified Theory of Time that would allow instantaneous communication across interstellar distances (the ansible, which appears in Le Guin's other work). The physics is a metaphor for the novel's actual subject: whether connection is possible across difference without one side consuming the other.

A few things I marked:

- The concept of "mutual aid" as practised on Anarres holds up but the novel is honest that it can curdle into conformism.
- Shevek's conversation with the Urrasti economist in chapter 9 is the clearest statement of the anarchist critique — that ownership of the means of production requires a state to enforce it.
- The ending is more ambiguous than it looks on first reading.

Length: 341 pages in the Panther paperback edition. Dense in places but never padded.
