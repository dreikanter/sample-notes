# Book review — "The Buried Giant" by Kazuo Ishiguro

Ishiguro's 2015 novel is set in post-Arthurian Britain, a world where a mysterious mist has caused collective forgetting. An elderly couple, Axl and Beatrice, journey to find their adult son, and the narrative slowly reveals what lies beneath the fog — both literally and metaphorically.

The novel has been criticized for being "too slow" and "not enough plot." These criticisms miss the point. Ishiguro is interested in the question of whether forgetting can preserve peace, and whether collective memory is a moral good in itself. The mist is a gift and a theft simultaneously — it prevents reconciliation but also prevents revenge.

The Arthurian frame is deliberately deteriorated. Merlin is old and failing. The Round Table's legacy is ambiguous at best. The legendary Britain has subsided into something ordinary and struggling. The effect is of myth viewed from a great distance, losing its gloss.

**The central question:**

Axl and Beatrice are devoted to each other but can't remember most of their life together. When Beatrice asks, "Will you still love me when we remember?" the novel reveals itself. Memory and identity and love are knotted together in ways the story takes seriously.

The ending is not easy and I don't think it's meant to be. Some readers have found it cruel. I found it honest.

Ishiguro's Nobel lecture, which is relevant to this book: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/2017/ishiguro/lecture/

His best novel? I still think "The Remains of the Day." But "The Buried Giant" has haunted me longer.
