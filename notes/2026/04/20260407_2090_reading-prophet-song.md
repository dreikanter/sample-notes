# Reading notes: Prophet Song

Paul Lynch, *Prophet Song* (2023). Booker Prize winner. Listed in my year-end reading (see [[20251229_2020]]); returning for proper notes.

## What the book does

Ireland, near-present, sliding toward fascism. The government has enabled a security force with expanding extra-legal powers; arrests begin. The novel follows Eilish, a scientist and mother of four, as the state gradually unmakes her family.

Lynch writes in dense, rushing sentences that rarely use paragraph breaks or standard dialogue punctuation. A page might contain dialogue, interior monologue, and event without formal separation. This creates a breathless quality — the world is happening to Eilish faster than she can process it, and we're in that same position.

## What I found difficult

The relentlessness. By the second half, the compression of dread has no relief. The book earns this — you understand why there's no relief — but it's the kind of novel you need to put down every 40 pages to let the pressure equalize.

## What the form is arguing

The rushed sentences, the lack of white space, the claustrophobic structure: Lynch is saying this is how it happens. Not with dramatic announcements but with paperwork and incremental normalizations, each one slightly worse than the last. The form enacts the slow-boiling quality of democratic backsliding. By the time you understand what's happening, so much has already happened.

## The refugee parallel

Eilish's sister-in-law is from the Middle East; we hear through her experience what Ireland has looked like to refugees for decades. The parallel is explicit and earned.
