# The remains of the day — reading notes

Finished Ishiguro's novel last night. It's one of those books where the prose style does most of the argumentative work, and I found myself underlining sentences not because they were quotable but because they demonstrated something about how Stevens narrates his own life to himself.

The central mechanism is Stevens's insistence on "dignity" in butlering, which he defines so narrowly that it ends up excluding most of what makes a life liveable. He treats dignity as professional composure and suppression of self — and the novel quietly shows what that costs him over thirty-five years. By the time he reaches Weymouth and his conversation with Miss Kenton (now Mrs Benn) crystallises what he gave up, the reader has already understood it far better than he has.

What's interesting is that Stevens isn't a fool or a monster. He's extremely good at his job, thoughtful about his craft in ways that are genuinely admirable, and capable of recognising his own rationalisation to a limited degree. Ishiguro doesn't make him easy to dismiss.

The embedded politics — Lord Darlington's appeasement sympathies, the Jewish maids — could feel heavy-handed but don't, because the novel is never really about politics. Darlington is there to show what it costs to serve someone uncritically, without independent moral judgment.

The unreliable narrator device here is subtler than Ishiguro's later work (cf. Never Let Me Go). Stevens doesn't lie to the reader exactly; he just withholds the emotional weight of what he's describing, and Ishiguro trusts you to feel it yourself.

Booker Prize 1989. Worth reading in parallel with a piece on postwar English class anxiety. The Guardian ran a good retrospective: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2015/jun/19/100-best-novels-the-remains-of-the-day-kazuo-ishiguro
