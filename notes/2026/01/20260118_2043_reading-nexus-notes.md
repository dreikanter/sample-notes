# Reading notes: Nexus

Yuval Noah Harari, *Nexus: A Brief History of Information Networks from the Stone Age to AI* (2024). In the year-end list (see [[20251229_2020]]), but coming back for proper notes.

The book's central thesis: human history is not primarily a story of technology but of information networks — the webs of shared stories, institutions, and communications that allow cooperation at scale. Every new information technology (writing, printing, internet, AI) doesn't just change what we know; it changes who we are as a collective entity.

**What's new here**: Harari's argument about AI as a third kind of entity. Previous information technologies (writing, printing) were tools — passive conduits. AI is an *agent* that makes decisions within networks, not just a medium. This changes the nature of the threat: you can regulate what people say in the press; it's harder to regulate what an autonomous agent chooses to surface, weight, or act on.

**Best chapter**: The section on the "Naive View of Information" — the Enlightenment assumption that more information leads naturally to more truth and better decisions. The 20th century is a pretty thorough refutation of this. Propaganda, disinformation, and algorithmic amplification all work *through* information, not against it.

**Where it's thinner**: The prescriptions. After 300 pages of diagnosis, the treatment section feels like well-meaning gestures toward "democratic oversight" without operational specifics. But prescription is genuinely hard here.

Worth reading, especially chapters 4–7.
