# Book notes: James by Percival Everett

Percival Everett, *James* (2024). Winner of the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction. Already listed it as my best read of 2025 (see [[20251229_2020]]). Coming back for proper notes.

## The concept

Jim — the enslaved man from *Huckleberry Finn* — retold from his own perspective, renamed James (his actual name, he tells us; "Jim" is the diminutive imposed on him). We learn that enslaved people in this world have developed a secret language and register-shifting practice: they speak "dumb" when white people are present and reveal their actual intelligence and inner lives only among themselves.

The device does enormous work. It frames the entire Twain original as a misreading — as Huck's limited, well-meaning, but ultimately white perspective on a man who was always smarter, more perceptive, and more complicated than the text allowed.

## What the novel is actually doing

Everett is interrogating the literary tradition of Black characters as seen through white protagonists. James's interiority is rich, philosophical, and politically aware. He has been reading Voltaire and Locke. He has opinions. He is not a device for Huck's moral education.

The novel's form enacts its argument: giving James a fully rendered inner life is the argument. You don't need the essays; the novel is the essay.

## Passage that stayed with me

James in the first chapter, shifting into "slave speech" when a white person approaches mid-sentence: the seam between his registers is invisible to the white character and completely legible to us. We're watching a performance; we're watching survival.
