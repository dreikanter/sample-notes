# Starling murmuration at the reservoir

Went out to the reservoir just before dusk on a hunch and caught a proper murmuration — probably the last one of the season before the birds disperse. It lasted around twenty-five minutes from first formation to roost.

What struck me this time, having read a bit about the mechanics beforehand, is how the shape never fully separates. Individual birds are only tracking their six or seven nearest neighbours according to the research, no central coordination, but the group holds together with a cohesion that looks intentional. The expansions and contractions propagate as waves across the whole flock in about a second regardless of flock size. At peak the group must have been four or five thousand birds.

The sound is strange — a dry rushing, like rain on a canvas tarp, but coming from above and moving. When they turned toward me the noise changed pitch because more wingbeats were directed in my direction. I hadn't expected that.

They eventually dropped into the reed bed on the south end in about forty seconds of accelerating spirals. Then silence except for the reedbed settling.

A 2010 paper from [Cavagna et al. in PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1005766107) covers the scale-free correlation finding — the idea that the correlation length between individual birds scales with flock size rather than being a fixed distance. That's the mechanism behind why the group behaves as a unit even when enormous.
