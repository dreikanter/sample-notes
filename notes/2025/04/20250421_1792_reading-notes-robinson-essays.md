# Reading notes — Marilynne Robinson, The Givenness of Things

Finished the Robinson essay collection. It took almost six weeks — not because it's long but because each essay requires sitting time afterward.

The collection is explicitly Christian and implicitly political. Robinson is defending a tradition of thought she feels is being abandoned or caricatured — the Calvinist strain in American intellectual history, the Puritan theological imagination (distinct from puritanism in the pejorative sense). She's correcting a historical record.

The essay "Grace" is the one I keep thinking about (mentioned briefly in [[20250315_1763]]). Her argument: grace is not primarily about doctrine but about a basic stance toward the world — a gratitude for existence that is prior to any theological proposition. That stance, she argues, produces a particular kind of attentiveness and generosity. The secular version, she thinks, is often just the same stance without the vocabulary.

The essay "Givenness" itself — about wonder and perception — connects to the Baker book (see [[20250321_1768]]) in a way I hadn't anticipated when I was reading them simultaneously. Baker's attention to the peregrines is, in Robinson's terms, a form of wonder that doesn't require naming itself as religious.

"Proofs" is the most contentious essay — a defense of the Puritans that will lose readers who come to it with the usual associations. But her point is that the historical Puritan thinkers were more sophisticated than the stereotype.

One of the more challenging and rewarding books I've read this year.
