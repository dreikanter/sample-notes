# Reading — Designing Data-Intensive Applications

Halfway through Kleppmann's DDIA. It's the most useful technical book I've read in several years, in part because it doesn't tell you what to use but explains the tradeoffs of different approaches so you can reason through your own situation.

The replication chapter (Chapter 5) is the section I'll re-read most often. Leader-follower replication, multi-leader, and leaderless replication are explained with enough detail to understand what can go wrong and why, not just what they're called. The treatment of replication lag and eventual consistency is clearer than anything else I've encountered.

The distributed systems section (Chapters 8-9) is where the book gets genuinely demanding. The treatment of the Two Generals Problem and the unreliable networks it leads to is lucid but not easy. Kleppmann's key point: distributed systems require you to reason about partial failures, which don't exist in single-node systems. Most of our mental models for "what can go wrong" don't extend naturally to networks.

Chapter 9 on consistency and consensus is the hardest going. Linearizability, causal consistency, sequential consistency — the distinctions matter but they require patience to work through. I read this section twice and I'm still not sure I fully understand the proofs around consensus algorithms.

What I appreciate most: Kleppmann is honest about what we don't have good solutions for. He describes the engineering tradeoffs as tradeoffs, not as solved problems.
