# On rereading

Finished *Piranesi* for the second time this month (first read was two years ago). The experience of rereading a mystery after knowing the solution is strange and underrated as a reading mode. You're no longer following a plot — you're watching the author construct one, noticing the exact places where the misdirection is built in.

What surprised me: the book is more emotionally coherent on second reading. The first time through, you're puzzling out the House, the tides, the statues. The second time, you know what Piranesi has lost, and every detail of his joyful competence in the House lands differently. His cataloguing habit, which reads as quirky on first read, is clearly a survival mechanism.

Nabokov wrote somewhere that real readers don't read, they reread — the idea being that first reads are for plot-following, which is an essentially passive activity, and rereading is where actual engagement begins. He's somewhat right, though this undersells how disorienting a good first reading can be, which has its own value.

I've been keeping a list of books worth rereading deliberately, rather than just books I liked. The criteria are different. *Invisible Cities* is on the list. *The Brothers Karamazov* is — see [[20250508_1011]] for some notes on the first reading.

There's an essay by Tim Parks on the ethics of rereading that covers some of this: https://www.nybooks.com/online/2014/10/02/why-we-read/ — his angle is about what we owe books vs. what we owe ourselves as readers.
