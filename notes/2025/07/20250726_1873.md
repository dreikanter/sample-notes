# Notes on coffee origins — Ethiopian naturals

Spent time this week reading about Ethiopian natural process coffees, prompted by a very good bag from a roaster I hadn't tried before.

Ethiopia is where Coffea arabica originated. The wild coffee forests of Kaffa, Jimma, and Harrar are still a source of genetic diversity for commercial breeding programs globally — Ethiopia's coffee forest biodiversity is an agricultural resource of global significance.

The natural (or dry) process means the coffee cherry is dried whole, with the fruit flesh still on the bean. This takes 3-6 weeks on raised drying beds, turned regularly to prevent fermentation going too far. The result: the dried fruit flesh imparts flavor compounds into the bean during drying. Natural process Ethiopians typically taste dramatically fruity — blueberry and strawberry are the most common descriptors, and they're not wrong.

This can go either way. A well-processed natural has intense fruit notes that are clean and complex. A poorly processed one tastes fermented and funky in an unpleasant way. There's a fine line between "wild and interesting" and "tastes like something went wrong."

The Yirgacheffe region produces what many consider the benchmark natural Ethiopians — both washed and natural processed coffees. The terroir (high altitude, heirloom varieties, specific microclimate) produces a distinctive character that shows even through different processing methods.

The bag I've been drinking: from Boru Lencha, a cooperative in Guji. Roasted light. The blueberry note is unmistakable.
