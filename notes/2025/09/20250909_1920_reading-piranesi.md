# Reading notes — Piranesi by Susanna Clarke

Finished on the train this morning. Around 270 pages, read across four sittings.

The central premise is quietly audacious: a man lives alone in a labyrinthine House whose halls are filled with statues and whose lower tiers flood with tides. He keeps a meticulous journal. He knows almost nothing about himself, but he knows the House intimately—its rhythms, its birds, its thirteen skeletons.

What Clarke does best here is world-building through negative space. The House is never explained; its geometry is impossible and the reader just accepts it the way Piranesi does, because his voice is so guileless and methodical. He catalogs. He measures. He is grateful for everything. The emotional weight of the novel comes from watching him be gentle with a world that, it turns out, was not gentle with him.

The mystery plot unfolds carefully without feeling engineered. I guessed some of the reveals but not all, and the ones I didn't guess landed well.

The title is an obvious nod to Giovanni Battista Piranesi, the 18th-century Italian printmaker known for his fantastical [Carceri d'Invenzione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_Prisons) engravings of impossible prisons. Clarke's House shares their vertiginous logic.

Comparisons to Borges are warranted but not complete—Clarke's affect is warmer, more domestic. The House wants to be loved and Piranesi loves it. That's the book.

Rating: 9/10. Will reread.
