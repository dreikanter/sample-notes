# Autumn equinox — a brief note

The equinox was September 22 this year, but I notice the change in the quality of the light more than the day length. By the time the equinox arrives, the light has already been slanting for weeks. What the equinox marks is the point at which we're losing daylight faster than any other time of year—the change accelerates around the equinoxes and slows at the solstices.

This is one of those pieces of astronomical trivia that I find unexpectedly affecting. We're at maximum rate of change, like the steepest part of a hill.

The temperature lagged behind the light by about three weeks this year. By late September the evenings were finally cool enough to warrant a heavier coat, and the leaves are beginning to turn, starting with the large sugar maple across the street, which goes first every year. I've watched it for three autumns now and it's reliable in a way that feels meaningful even though it isn't.

I like autumn better than any other season. I know people say this and it's unfashionable because it's so common, but the honest response to unfashionable commonality is to ask why so many people feel the same thing—not to pretend to be above it.

The [NOAA explanation of equinoxes and solstices](https://www.noaa.gov/) is a good twenty minutes if you're curious about the mechanics.

The light this morning was low and amber by 7 a.m. and I was glad to be awake for it.
