# Reading notes — The Craftsman by Richard Sennett

**Author:** Richard Sennett
**Published:** 2008, Yale University Press

About 250 pages in, with perhaps 80 to go. Dense but rewarding.

Sennett's argument is that craftsmanship—understood broadly as the desire to do a job well for its own sake—is a fundamental human impulse that modern institutions systematically suppress. He traces this through examples ranging from medieval guilds to the Linux open-source community.

The chapter on resistance and the hand was most interesting to me. Sennett argues that skilled craftspeople learn from material resistance—wood that doesn't respond as expected, a pot that cracks in the kiln—in ways that are cognitively irreplaceable. You can't learn the same thing from a simulation because simulation removes the resistance that generates knowledge.

This connects to a current debate in software about whether AI-assisted code generation will erode deep programming knowledge the way GPS has eroded navigation ability. The parallel isn't perfect but it's there.

Less convincing: the sections on architecture, where Sennett is critical of Haussmann's Paris and the anonymous glass tower but doesn't quite acknowledge how much of his aesthetic preferences are doing the philosophical work.

The section on the Linux kernel as a craft community is dated now but still interesting historically. He interviewed kernel contributors in the mid-2000s, before the growth of GitHub and the shift in open-source norms.

More on this when I finish. The [Yale press page](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/book/9780300151190/the-craftsman/) has additional context.
