# On reading slowly

There's a piece of received wisdom about reading that I've been trying to actively push against this year: the assumption that reading faster is reading better.

I read quickly by habit. Academic training reinforces this — skimming for argument, reading footnotes selectively, getting the gist. This is efficient for certain purposes. For literature, it's actively bad.

Reading Chekhov slowly [[20250607_1845]] — one story per day, returning to passages, not moving on until something landed — was qualitatively different from my usual mode. The stories are short enough that you can hold the whole shape in mind while reading a single sentence. The ending of "The Lady with the Dog" changes what the opening means; reading slowly lets you feel that happen.

Slow reading has a physical component that I don't fully understand. When I read quickly, my eyes move across the page in a particular scanning way that doesn't feel quite like full comprehension. When I read slowly — sometimes reading a sentence aloud, or pausing and looking up before continuing — there's a different quality of attention.

I've started using a simple rule: if I'm reading something and I realize I couldn't summarize the last paragraph in a sentence, I go back. The failure mode of quick reading is covering distance without absorbing anything.

What I can't quite figure out: whether slow reading means simply spending more time on the same material, or whether the quality of attention is genuinely different at different speeds. The evidence from my own experience suggests it's the latter.
