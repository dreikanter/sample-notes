# Book notes — The Dispossessed by Ursula K. Le Guin

Finished this last night. First time reading Le Guin despite meaning to for years.

The structure is alternating chapters: one timeline follows the physicist Shevek as he travels from his home moon Anarres to the wealthy planet Urras, the other traces his life on Anarres in reverse chronological order. The two timelines converge at the novel's center.

Anarres is an anarchist society—propertyless, stateless, organized around voluntary cooperation and mutual criticism. Urras is essentially a stand-in for Cold War Earth, complete with capitalist and communist superpowers. The comparison is not subtle, but Le Guin is interested in showing both sides honestly, which she does: Anarres is not a utopia; it has its own social pressures, jealousies, and conformities.

What I found most interesting is the treatment of language. Anarres uses Pravic, a constructed language that eliminates possessives and changes how ownership can even be thought. Le Guin sketches this mostly through implication rather than long explanations, which is the right choice.

The physics in the novel—Shevek is developing a unified theory of time—is handwaved in the way all fictional physics is, but the emotional logic of his work is precise: the idea that simultaneity and sequence are both real and both incomplete.

Strong recommendation. The [Le Guin website](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/) has extensive archival material.

Rating: 9/10. Especially if you read it alongside something on political philosophy.
