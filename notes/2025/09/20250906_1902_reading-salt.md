# Reading notes — Salt by Mark Kurlansky

Kurlansky's approach — taking a single commodity and tracing its role in human history — works here better than I expected. Salt is a more interesting organizing principle than it sounds because it's been used as currency, tax base, military advantage, and food preservation technology across every civilization.

The most surprising material: the role of salt in the preservation of fish, which made the long-distance trade in dried and salted cod possible, which made European exploration of the North Atlantic economically viable, which shaped the settlement of North America and the economics of the slave trade (salt cod was a significant part of the triangular trade). A line of causation you wouldn't have expected to run through a condiment.

The chapter on salt taxes — particularly the French *gabelle* — is genuinely interesting. The *gabelle* was among the most hated taxes in pre-revolutionary France, partly for its rate but mostly for its arbitrariness: the tax rate varied by region in ways that made no sense, and tax farmers (private tax collectors) enforced it brutally. The injustice of salt taxation features in the list of grievances that fed the revolution.

The section on China is the weakest — less detailed than the European material, which reflects the limits of Kurlansky's sources.

The book has a food history strand alongside the political history, with historical salt-based recipes interspersed. These are charming and impractical.

Good for reading in small sections rather than straight through.
