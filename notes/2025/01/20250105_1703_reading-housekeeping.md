# Reading notes — Housekeeping

Marilynne Robinson, 1980. First novel. Finished.

The comparison to Woolf is right but incomplete. Robinson shares Woolf's attention to how light and water and the physical world carry meaning, and her sentences have the same quality of sustained attention. But where Woolf's prose tends toward the associative and fragmentary, Robinson's has an almost biblical stateliness. Sentences that begin and end with authority.

The novel follows two sisters in a small Idaho town on a lake where their father, and later grandfather, drowned. Their guardian is their aunt Sylvie, who is a transient — someone who drifts, sleeps in parks, is more comfortable outside than in. Sylvie's relationship to housekeeping (the literal domestic kind) is a refusal, and the novel asks what that refusal means.

The central tension: what does it mean to preserve a self and a life versus letting them drift and dissolve? Sylvie represents the claim that dissolution might be preferable — that permanence is a kind of violence against the natural order of things. Ruth, the narrator, is drawn toward this view and frightened by it.

The lake is always present. The drowned are present. Memory in the novel is not the comfortable preservation it is in most narratives — it's more like flooding. Things from the past don't stay in the past; they come back up.

The ending is radical. I won't describe it but it refuses easy consolation and I found it satisfying in the way that a mathematical proof is satisfying — it follows from everything that came before, inescapably.

Better than the Gilead trilogy, I think, or at least stranger and less careful about what it asks of the reader. The [interview with Robinson in The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2012/jun/01/marilynne-robinson-absence-solitude-gilead) covers some of this.
