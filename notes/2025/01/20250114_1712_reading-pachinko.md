# Reading notes — Pachinko

Min Jin Lee, 2017. A multigenerational saga following a Korean family across four generations, beginning in Japanese-occupied Korea in 1910 and ending in Japan in 1989.

The scope is ambitious. Lee is tracking what it means to be Zainichi (ethnic Korean living in Japan) over nearly a century — a population that occupied a legally ambiguous and socially despised position in Japanese society for generations, many of whose members were born in Japan but were not Japanese citizens and could not become so.

What makes the novel more than a genre saga is Lee's refusal to make the suffering redemptive in an easy way. The families endure; they also fail each other, make bad decisions, carry prejudices that damage the next generation. The pachinko business that gives the novel its title is itself a microcosm — socially stigmatised work that the Zainichi were pushed toward and that many of them built into legitimate wealth. The moral status of that path is never resolved.

Individual characters are well-drawn enough to feel continuous across the time jumps. Sunja, the matriarch, is not a saint but she has a kind of clear-eyed endurance that is more interesting than sainthood.

The [Min Jin Lee interview with NPR](https://www.npr.org/2017/02/07/513767059/pachinko-is-a-story-about-koreans-in-japan-and-the-american-dream) gives good context on the research — Lee spent nearly 30 years gathering material.

A book that makes you want to know more history. I've been reading about the Zainichi situation since finishing it — the history is genuinely less known outside Japan than it deserves to be.
