# January cooking philosophy

January cooking is a different mode. The richness of December — the fat roasts, the puddings, the elaborate sauces — gives way to something leaner and more honest. I don't experience this as deprivation; it feels more like a return to a register I find more naturally interesting.

What January calls for:

**Legumes**: lentils in every form. Green lentil soup with preserved lemon and cumin. Puy lentils with roasted beetroot and goats cheese. Dal makhani that's been simmering for two hours [[20241122_1659]]. The slow, sustaining quality of beans and lentils suits the month.

**Root vegetables**: what's actually in season in Britain in January. Celeriac, swede, parsnip, beetroot, winter squash if the stored ones haven't gone off. Roasting root vegetables at high heat is one of the most reliably satisfying things in cold weather cooking — the caramelisation, the warmth, the transformation from raw density to something tender and concentrated.

**Broth and stock**: January is when the freezer stock bags get used. Turkey broth, chicken stock from last Sunday's bird. The foundation of a week of soups and braises.

**Simple pasta**: not the showpiece pasta of entertaining but weeknight pasta — aglio e olio, cacio e pepe, pasta with cannellini beans and rosemary. Food that takes fifteen minutes and asks nothing of you.

**Bitter leaves**: radicchio, chicory, Belgian endive. The bitterness is what the palate wants after December sweetness. The [River Café cookbook](https://rivercafe.co.uk/books) has a salad of radicchio, walnuts, and Gorgonzola that I've made every January for three years.

The principle: eat what's seasonal, cook simply, value ingredients over technique. It's the most sustainable way to cook well and the easiest on January budget and energy alike.
