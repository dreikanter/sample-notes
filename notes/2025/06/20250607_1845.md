# Notes on re-reading Chekhov stories

Spending a week reading through Chekhov stories I last read in university. The experience is quite different fifteen years later.

The story "The Lady with the Dog" reads now as a much sadder story than I remember it. The ending — where Gurov and Anna realize their love is real and acknowledge they still have far to go in their difficult complicated lives — felt hopeful the first time. Now it reads as a description of a life mostly already used up, with a late-arriving clarity that comes too late to do much with.

"Ward No. 6" is the one that surprised me most on re-reading. I had no memory of it. It's about a doctor who becomes a patient in the psychiatric ward he runs — a long, slow story about the dangers of passive philosophical acceptance. Chekhov was a physician himself and the medical detail is precise and unsentimental.

What strikes me across all the stories: the complete absence of authorial judgment. Chekhov gives you characters in situations and then declines to tell you what to think about them. The famous Chekhov note to a friend — "don't tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass" — is actually about more than technique. It's about refusing to shortcut the reader's experience.

The stories also aged better than I expected in terms of texture and detail. Small Russian provincial life in the 1880s-90s is remote but readable because the psychology is immediate.

Good introduction to Chekhov's technique: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2008/jan/19/classics.antonychekhov
