# Reading notes — The Warmth of Other Suns

**Author:** Isabel Wilkerson
**Published:** 2010

About 200 pages into this — a substantial way in but less than a third. The decision to structure the book around three specific people (Ida Mae Brandon, George Swanson Starling, and Robert Joseph Pershing Foster) is paying off. It gives texture to what could be an overwhelming statistical story.

The method Wilkerson uses: she interviews descendants and surviving participants, reads letters and newspapers of the period, and reconstructs scenes with a novelist's specificity. A particular detail I've kept thinking about — Ida Mae's husband's cousin was beaten nearly to death over a few stolen turkeys, and the family's flight north followed within weeks. Cause-and-effect, but not neat cause-and-effect.

The book covers migrations from the 1910s through the 1970s. What I find most striking is how recently a lot of this is. George Starling was alive when Wilkerson interviewed him. The people who experienced Jim Crow systematically and fled it are not distant ancestors—they're the grandparents and parents of people alive today.

The [Pulitzer Prize citation](https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/isabel-wilkerson) for the book notes the "rigorous research and masterly narrative." The research is visible without being burdensome—you can tell she's done enormous work because the book doesn't feel the need to show it constantly.

Taking this one slowly. The weight of it accumulates.
