# Cinema notes: Jeanne Dielman, 23 quai du Commerce, 1080 Bruxelles

Finally watched Chantal Akerman's 1975 film in full — three and a half hours, nearly in real time. The premise is relentlessly ordinary: a widowed Brussels housewife runs her household over three days, caring for her teenage son and receiving one male client per day as a quiet economic arrangement. Akerman shoots the domestic routines at waist height with a static camera, forcing the viewer to sit inside the duration of peeling potatoes, washing dishes, buttoning a coat.

The effect is strange. By the second day you notice the rhythm has fractured slightly. Potatoes aren't drained quickly enough; a pot lid is left slightly off-center; a button takes too long. These are almost subliminal signals that something is deteriorating internally. No dialogue explains it.

What the film seems to argue — or at least demonstrate — is that the aestheticization of women's domestic labor in cinema usually works by compressing or eliding it. Show two minutes of washing dishes with warm light and a music cue and it becomes charming. Show twenty minutes in real time and it becomes something else entirely: labor, repetition, time.

Some film scholars read it as a feminist critique of housework's invisibility in political economy; others frame it through phenomenology and duration. Both readings feel accurate simultaneously.

The Criterion release includes an essay by B. Ruby Rich worth reading before the film: https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/168-jeanne-dielman-23-quai-du-commerce-1080-bruxelles

Reminded me obliquely of Borges on the infinite and repetition — the way iteration without variation eventually becomes its own kind of event. [[20230719_1005]]
