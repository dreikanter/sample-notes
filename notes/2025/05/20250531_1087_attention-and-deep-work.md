# Attention and deep work

Read Newport's *Deep Work* late, which means I came to it having already absorbed most of its ideas secondhand through blog posts and productivity Twitter. The actual book is denser and more specific than the secondhand version suggests.

The distinction between deep and shallow work is clearer than I expected. Deep work is cognitively demanding, creates new value, and is hard to replicate. Shallow work is logistical, low cognitive load, easy to replicate. The problem Newport identifies is that shallow work expands to fill available time because it feels productive — email, Slack, quick reviews — while being largely interchangeable.

The scheduling blocks experiment: I ran four weeks of strict 90-minute deep blocks from 8–9:30 AM, calendar blocked, notifications off. The first week was uncomfortable. By week three I was producing first drafts of documentation in a single session that used to take two scattered afternoons.

What Newport doesn't quite address is the social friction cost. Being unreachable for 90 minutes in a job that involves coordination means someone else absorbs the interruption. The efficiency gain isn't free; it's partly redistributed.

His discussion of craftsman identity (treat attention as a skill to be built, not a resource to be protected) resonates more than the scheduling tactics. https://www.calnewport.com/books/deep-work/ for the official summary.

Counterpoint worth reading: https://www.newyorker.com/culture/cultural-comment/is-email-making-professors-stupid — argues the problem is structural, not individual.
