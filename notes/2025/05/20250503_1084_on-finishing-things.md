# On finishing things

There's a distinction I've been trying to sharpen between stopping work and finishing it. They look the same from the outside — you put down the tool, you close the file — but they feel completely different and produce different results.

Stopping is when the time runs out or the energy does. The work is wherever it is when you leave it. Finishing is a deliberate act: you reach a state where the current phase is complete, even if more phases follow. It might just mean writing a single line that summarizes where you are and what the next step would be, but that act changes the object. It's now a thing that could be picked up rather than a thing that would have to be excavated.

I notice this most with writing. Articles I stopped mid-draft are almost impossible to return to. The sentence I was in the middle of becomes a barrier because I've lost the specific thought it was tracking. But if I stopped at a section boundary and left a note — even "next section covers X, needs example from the Paris thing" — I can re-enter the work within a few minutes.

The habit is not natural. The instinct when energy drops is to stop immediately. Finishing requires spending a small amount of effort at the moment you have the least of it.

This connects to something in the [Getting Things Done](https://gettingthingsdone.com/) system I've always appreciated but always under-practiced: the idea that the next action needs to be defined, not just the project. The project name is the excavation problem. The specific next action is the pickup point.

I'm not sure this is about productivity exactly. It feels more like a form of respect for the future self who will be doing the picking up.
