# Reading notes — Piranesi by Susanna Clarke

Finished this in two sittings. It's a compact novel — under 300 pages — but it manages to do something that most longer books don't: the central mystery is revealed in stages that each genuinely reorient your understanding of what you've read.

The House is the novel's great achievement. Clarke describes it as an infinite space of marble halls, statues, tides, and clouds — all coexisting within a single building. It sounds absurd on paper. On the page it feels entirely real, because Piranesi (the narrator) describes it with the practical precision of someone who has lived there long enough to know which staircases flood at high tide and where to find fresh fish after a storm.

The prose is deceptively plain. Piranesi writes in a notebook — journal entries with dates measured by his own peculiar calendar. The simplicity of the style is itself a clue. His vocabulary suggests someone intelligent but also someone whose worldview has been deliberately narrowed.

What stays with me: the way the novel treats wonder not as naivety but as a kind of rigorous attention. Piranesi marvels at statues, at tidal patterns, at the behavior of birds. It's methodical wonder, not sentimental. There's something almost Stoic about it.

Clarke's influences seem to include C.S. Lewis and Jorge Luis Borges. The House as a space echoes Borges' Library of Babel. See also her interview: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2020/sep/12/susanna-clarke-piranesi-interview

A genuinely strange and beautiful book. Recommended without reservation.
