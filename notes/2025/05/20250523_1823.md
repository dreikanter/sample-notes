# Film review — Perfect Days (2023)

Wim Wenders' film about a Tokyo toilet cleaner is exactly what it says on the poster: quiet, measured, and unexpectedly moving. Hirayama (Koji Yakusho) wakes at the same time each morning, waters his seedlings, buys coffee from a vending machine, and drives to work with cassette tapes on the stereo. The routine is not presented as trapped repetition but as something chosen, even curated.

What Wenders does well here is resist explanation. We learn almost nothing about Hirayama's past — a sister visits once and there's a half-scene with a niece that implies some family history, but it's never spelled out. The film trusts the present moment over backstory, which is consistent with the Zen-adjacent worldview it's circling.

The photography of light through leaves is a recurring motif. Hirayama photographs it — actually photographs it, on analog film — and the shots are spare and beautiful. It's a bit on-the-nose as symbolism but it works because Yakusho plays it without affect.

The supporting characters are less interesting, especially the young colleague who functions mainly as a foil to show how unusual Hirayama's contentment is. This is a flaw but a small one.

Lou Reed's "Perfect Day" plays at the end. It's the least subtle moment in a film that is otherwise admirably restrained.

Official Wenders interview: https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/interviews/wim-wenders-perfect-days

Rating: 4/5. A film for afternoons when you want to feel steadied rather than moved.
