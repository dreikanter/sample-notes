# Reading Le Guin's carrier bag theory

Ursula K. Le Guin's 1986 essay "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" runs to only a few pages but it has been sitting with me for days. The central provocation: what if the first human tool was not the spear but the bag — the container that holds gathered food, seeds, small things? And what does it mean for storytelling if we take that seriously?

Le Guin borrows the container idea from Elizabeth Fisher and uses it to argue against the Hero's Journey as the default shape of narrative. The spear story is linear, climactic, and built around a single protagonist's triumph. The carrier bag story is accumulative, messy, full of things that don't all point the same direction.

She's not saying action is bad. She's saying the weapon-as-first-tool mythology shapes which stories we consider worth telling. You can find the essay in the collection [Dancing at the Edge of the World](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/159916/dancing-at-the-edge-of-the-world-by-ursula-k-le-guin/).

What I keep thinking about is how this maps onto nonfiction. A lot of long-form journalism is still organized around the spear: the revelation, the confrontation, the resolution. Pieces that resist that structure — accumulating details, holding contradictions open — often get described as "sprawling" or "undisciplined" by editors who mean it as a criticism.

I want to test this lens against a few books I have queued. The Borges reading from a while back [[20230719_1005]] felt more like a carrier bag than a spear, in retrospect. Things held together by adjacency, not causality.

Le Guin's ending is worth quoting directly: "It is a strange realism, but it is a strange reality."
