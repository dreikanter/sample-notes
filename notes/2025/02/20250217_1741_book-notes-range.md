# Book notes — Range by David Epstein

Picked this up from the backlog (see [[20250207_1730]]). About 60% through.

The central argument: in complex, uncertain domains, breadth of experience often predicts success better than early specialization. Epstein contrasts "kind" learning environments (chess, golf — where feedback is immediate and patterns are stable) with "wicked" learning environments (medicine, science, business — where rules are unclear and feedback is delayed or misleading). Specialists thrive in kind environments; generalists tend to outperform in wicked ones.

The Tiger Woods vs Roger Federer opening works well as a hook. Woods was specialized early; Federer sampled broadly before settling on tennis. Both are exceptional, but Epstein argues Federer's path is more generalizable as a model than Woods's, despite the common advice to specialize early.

Chapter 5 on research scientists is the most convincing part. Scientists who work across multiple domains are more likely to produce innovative research. Narrow specialization produces incremental work within existing frameworks.

Where the book is weaker: some of the case studies prove the point by selection. You don't hear about the generalists who tried many things and excelled at none. The base rate problem.

Still useful. The section on "interleaved practice" vs "blocked practice" in learning is directly applicable to how I think about skill-building — mixing up problem types, even at the cost of short-term performance, improves long-term retention.

Author's website and reviews: [https://davidepstein.com/the-range/](https://davidepstein.com/the-range/)
