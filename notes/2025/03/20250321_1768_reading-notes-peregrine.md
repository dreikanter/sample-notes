# Reading notes — The peregrine by J.A. Baker

From the backlog (see [[20250207_1730]]). Baker's book is from 1967 and describes a winter spent tracking peregrine falcons across the Essex countryside in England. It is one of the strangest nature books I've encountered.

The prose is extraordinarily concentrated. Baker often describes the same kinds of events over and over — hawk finds prey, hawk stoops, hawk kills or misses — but each description is written freshly, as if Baker is determined to exhaust the event of all its meaning before moving on. The repetition becomes hypnotic rather than tedious.

There is almost no personal information in the book. We don't know Baker's circumstances, his age, what he did for work. He appears as a presence in the landscape, following the hawks, and nothing more. This self-erasure creates an odd intimacy — the reader is very close to the observer while knowing nothing about him.

The hawks themselves are described with unnerving precision. Baker clearly knew them as individuals over time and could read their behavior, moods, hunting strategies. The descriptions of stoops (the falcon's diving attack) are the best prose I've read about animal movement — fast but precise, full of aerodynamic specificity without sacrificing the sense of wildness.

Last chapter is melancholy. The peregrines were declining due to DDT at the time of writing. Baker documents their absence at the end of winter.

I thought about this in relation to [[20250306_1755]] and the hawk I possibly saw. Baker's precision makes me want to pay more attention to what I actually see.
