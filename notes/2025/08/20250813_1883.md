# Podcast — Ezra Klein interview with Derek Thompson on abundance

Listened to this interview twice — it's worth the time. Thompson argues that many of the structural problems in American life (housing costs, healthcare costs, slow infrastructure, energy transition) have a common cause: a culture that has become much better at blocking things than building them.

The book (*Abundance*) covers well-traveled territory about NIMBYism and permitting but the framing is useful. Thompson and Klein's argument is that progressivism has developed a strand that prioritizes process and opposition over outcomes. Environmental review, historic preservation, community input, equity impact assessments — all valuable in principle — have accumulated into a system where building anything is nearly impossible regardless of its merit.

What I found most interesting: the comparison to the post-war period, when the US built interstate highways, the electrical grid, the Hoover Dam, and large amounts of public housing at speed. Some of that was bad (urban highway routing that destroyed communities, for example). But the speed was real, and it was enabled by a different relationship to centralized decision-making and a different calculus about tradeoffs.

The proposed solution is essentially institutional reform: shorter timelines, streamlined appeals, changing the default from "no until proven yes" to "yes unless proven no." This is politically difficult because the coalition that would have to enact it includes many people who benefit from the current blocking capacity.

I'm skeptical that the problem is primarily cultural rather than structural, but the diagnosis is useful even if the prescription is incomplete.
