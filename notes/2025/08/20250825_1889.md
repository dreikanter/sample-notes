# Reading — The Ministry for the Future

Kim Stanley Robinson's novel about climate change is formally unusual: it reads partly like a novel, partly like an economics textbook, partly like a documentary. Chapters alternate between a conventional narrative following Mary Murphy (head of the fictional UNFCCC body of the title) and short documentary-style chapters covering carbon currencies, central bank intervention, carbon capture technology, and political violence.

The opening chapter is devastating and intentionally so — a mass heat-death event in India, thousands of people dying in a wet bulb temperature event that makes survival without air conditioning impossible. It's presented with clinical directness. This is the novel's argument in its rawest form: this is what's coming; here is why it's happening; here is how it might be averted.

The economic imagination is the most interesting part of the book. Robinson has done considerable research into heterodox economics — the carbon coin proposal (a currency backed by sequestered carbon, issued by central banks) is a serious policy idea that he dramatizes rather than invents. The novel takes seriously the idea that the financial system, if properly redirected, is the lever that could actually move emissions fast enough.

What doesn't work: the conventional narrative is the weakest element. Mary Murphy and Frank May (an American survivor of the opening disaster) are vehicles for the ideas rather than fully realized characters. Robinson's strength is speculation, not characterization.

The ending is more hopeful than the premise suggests it should be. Whether that hope is earned or wishful is the question the book is asking you to decide.
