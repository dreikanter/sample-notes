# Reading notes — The master and Margarita

Bulgakov, completed 1940, published in censored form 1966, complete form 1973. I've been working through this slowly since late April (mentioned in [[20240420_1490]]).

The novel interweaves three narratives: Pontius Pilate interrogating Yeshua Ha-Nozri (a Jesus figure) in ancient Jerusalem; Satan (Woland) and his entourage visiting Soviet Moscow in the 1930s and causing mayhem; and the love story of the Master (an author) and Margarita. These become braided rather than separate by the final third.

The Moscow sections are a sustained satire on Soviet cultural bureaucracy — the residents of the literary association, the housing committee officials, the psychiatric system that swallows anyone who reports the supernatural events. The comedy here is pitch-black because the target was the actual system Bulgakov was living under when he wrote it. The devil's visit exposes the residents not through magic but by showing what their actual values are when given the opportunity to express them: greed, self-preservation, petty ambition.

The Pilate sections are more purely literary — a meditation on power, cowardice, and conscience. Pilate knows Yeshua is innocent and lets him be executed anyway. This is presented as his irremediable flaw: the moment he chose safety over justice.

Margarita is the most sympathetic and perhaps most fully realized character. Her deal with the devil is made out of love and she holds to it without compromise.

Translation matters: the Glenny translation I have is good; the Pevear/Volokhonsky is apparently preferred by many.
