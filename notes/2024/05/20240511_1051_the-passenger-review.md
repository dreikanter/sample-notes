# Brief review of "The Passenger" by Cormac McCarthy

Finished *The Passenger* a few nights ago. It's a difficult book to land on.

The setup: Bobby Western, a salvage diver and former race-car driver, pulls a body from a sunken plane off the Gulf Coast. A passenger is missing from the manifest. Men start appearing to ask him questions. His sister, Alicia — a mathematical prodigy who died by suicide ten years earlier — haunts his memory through long, hallucinatory interludes.

The problem is that these two threads are in tension in a way that never quite resolves. The thriller plot about the missing passenger and the federal agents quietly evaporates. The grief narrative and the philosophical dialogues between Bobby and Alicia's hallucinations are where McCarthy is actually working, but those sections are also the most self-indulgent. The "Thalidomide Kid" sequences are striking in their strangeness but go on longer than they earn.

What stays with me is the portrait of a man who has opted out — who has chosen diminishment deliberately, not from failure but from something closer to exhaustion with wanting. That's interesting territory. McCarthy earns a few moments of genuine weight in that register.

Compare it to *The Road*, which achieves something similar with far greater economy. Worth reading if you're working through McCarthy's late work, but it's not where I'd start. [The NYRB review by Christian Lorentzen](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2022/11/17/no-country-for-young-men-cormac-mccarthy/) gets at the book's ambitions better than most.
