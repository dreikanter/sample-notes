# Film review — The Zone of Interest

Jonathan Glazer's film is the most formally controlled piece of cinema I've seen in years, and also the most disturbing. The formal control is the point.

The premise: Rudolf Höss, commandant of Auschwitz, and his family live in a house with a garden next to the camp. The film shows the domestic life — children playing, the wife tending flowers, dinner parties, swimming, ordinary days. The camp is present only as sound: trains, gunshots, cries, the constant mechanical background of industrial murder.

The technique is surveillance footage aesthetics — multiple static cameras, no music, no conventional drama. The horror arrives from the gap between the visual normality and what the ear hears. You are never shown what is happening beyond the wall. You hear it for 105 minutes.

The effect accumulates rather than strikes. By the middle of the film, the sound has become a constant pressure, and watching the family inhabit their domestic world with such ease against it produces something close to nausea.

Glazer's formal bet is that showing nothing is more unbearable than showing something. He wins the bet.

The interspersed sequences with the young Polish girl leaving food for prisoners are filmed in infrared — a different world in a different light. They're the only scenes of resistance in the film, almost the only scenes of human recognition.

This is not a film that offers catharsis or moral clarity. It offers implication.

Rating: 10/10. [A24 release](https://a24films.com/films/the-zone-of-interest).
