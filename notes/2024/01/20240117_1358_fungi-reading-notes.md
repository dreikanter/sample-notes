# Reading notes — Entangled Life by Merlin Sheldrake

Stopped on page ninety to write some things down.

Sheldrake's argument about mycelial networks as a model of distributed cognition is the thing I'll think about the longest. Fungi have no brain, no nervous system, no center — and yet a mycelial network can "solve" the maze problem (find the shortest path to food through a maze) in ways that look like intelligent behavior. The network finds efficient paths through distributed sensing and chemical signaling.

The comparison to slime mold (Physarum polycephalum) is striking: researchers gave slime mold the same map layout as the Tokyo rail network, put food at nodes corresponding to major population centers, and the slime mold grew into something almost identical to the actual Tokyo metro system — including the redundancy and fault tolerance that human engineers designed in deliberately.

This is not intelligence in any sense that requires a self. It's information processing at a scale and distributed character that breaks the usual intuition about where thinking happens.

The mycorrhizal network section (the "wood wide web" popular science version, but more rigorously described) makes the case that forest trees share resources through fungal intermediaries — carbon from photosynthetically rich trees flowing to shaded trees, phosphorus moving in the other direction. The network isn't altruistic in any meaningful sense, but the outcome resembles altruism.

Questions I'm sitting with: What are the limits of the distributed cognition analogy? Where does it break down? The slime mold result is reproducible and striking, but the line between "behaves as if intelligent" and "is intelligent" seems to matter and also seems to dissolve on close examination.
