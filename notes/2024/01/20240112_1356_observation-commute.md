# Observation — the commute again

Started going into the office two days a week again after the holiday, and I'd forgotten the specific texture of the commute after months of working from home.

The train is full of people doing different kinds of nothing. One man reading a physical newspaper, which I haven't seen in two years. A teenager watching something with the sound off and the subtitles on, which is my preferred mode on trains too. Three people asleep. The number of screens active at once is still startling to me, not because I think it's bad but because it's new — this is the first generation that has a private entertainment infrastructure in their pocket during all dead time.

What I notice about my own commute: I can't write on the train. Too much ambient distraction. I can read but only nonfiction; fiction requires a stillness of mind I can't reach when there's movement and noise. I can think, and the commute has become the best place for it. Standing problems need movement.

There's a paradox in the fact that the office work I do when I'm actually there is less good than the office work I imagine doing when I'm home. The reverse also seems true. The best thinking happens in the transition between the two, on the train, in neither state.

A colleague who commutes an hour each way says she considers it protected time and uses it for podcasts and audiobooks. The frame matters — a commute resented is dead time; a commute claimed is something else.

Reference: [Rebecca Solnit's *Wanderlust*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/330126/wanderlust-by-rebecca-solnit/) on the relationship between walking, thinking, and moving through space.
