# Early notes — Seeing Like a State

Sixty pages in. The first chapter on how states "legible-ize" territory is the setup for everything that follows, and it's already rewiring how I think about administration and software systems simultaneously.

Scott's example of forest management in 18th-century Prussia is the key illustration. German foresters, trying to maximize timber yield, replaced complex mixed forests with orderly monoculture plantations. They could measure and predict yields. Everything was legible. For one generation, yields increased. Then the second-growth forest began to fail — the stripped soil, the eliminated understory ecology, the absence of the complex interdependencies that sustained the system. They had made the forest legible by destroying what made it a forest.

The concept he introduces is *metis* — practical, local, context-dependent knowledge that can't be reduced to explicit rules. The peasant farmer who knows by feel when the soil is ready to plow, the experienced nurse who can assess a patient before the chart is read. This knowledge is invisible to administrative systems that require everything to be measurable and transferable.

**The software engineering translation I keep making:**

When we extract and document a system, we often inadvertently destroy the implicit knowledge that lived in the people and practices surrounding it. The rewrite that removes "dead code" sometimes removes the code that handled the exception that will occur again in three years.

Legibility serves administrators. It can impoverish the system being administered.
