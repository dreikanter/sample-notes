# Museum visit — local natural history collection

Saturday afternoon, spontaneous. The natural history museum is twenty minutes away and I haven't been since I moved here. This was a mistake that took two years to correct.

## The mineral hall

The gem and mineral hall is the best room in the building. The specimens are lit individually against black velvet and the lighting is doing a lot of work — it catches the crystalline structure in fluorite, the metallic sheen in bismuth, the fibrous parallel structure in selenite. The didactic labels are minimal, which is either a failure or a choice; I chose to read it as a choice.

The bismuth specimens are the most dramatic — hopper-shaped crystals with iridescent surfaces that read as artificial. The iridescence is from a thin oxide layer, the same physics as a soap bubble or an oil slick.

## The taxidermy hall

Older taxidermy — probably mid-twentieth century — with the stiffness and slightly wrong eyes of that era. A mounted black bear. A wolverine that has not aged well. A large case of shore birds that is either excellent or haunting, depending on your mood.

The habitat dioramas have a particular quality: utterly still life captured in painted backgrounds and real specimens, a frozen moment in an ecosystem that no longer exists in that configuration. The diorama of a mixed hardwood forest has trees that were current-sized in 1960 and would be taller now.

## What I'll come back for

The fossil section, which I ran out of time for. The ammonite cases in the entrance hall were too good to be the whole collection.

Reference: [Museum's online collection](https://www.si.edu/) — the Smithsonian's digitized natural history collection for the specimens you can't see in person.
