# On meetings — what actually makes them useful

After the retrospective feedback about our planning meetings running long, I've been thinking more carefully about what distinguishes meetings that feel worth the time from ones that don't.

## The useful kinds of meetings

**Decision meetings:** Gather the right people, establish context briefly, make the decision, document it, leave. These need exactly the people with relevant information and authority — no more. They can be short.

**Creative or exploratory meetings:** Design sessions, brainstorming, architecture discussions. These need space to breathe. The problem with scheduling them for 30 minutes is that they require context-building and that takes time. Better to have fewer of these and give them an hour and a half.

**Relationship and calibration:** One-on-ones, check-ins. These can't be replaced by async because the point isn't information transfer — it's the quality of the working relationship. These deserve full attention, not half-attention while reading Slack.

## What makes most meetings bad

1. **No decision to be made.** Information that could be an email becomes a meeting because "it's complex." Usually it isn't that complex.

2. **Wrong attendees.** Too many people for a decision, or the one person who actually needs to make the call isn't there.

3. **No preparation.** People showing up without having read the pre-read and then having the meeting reconstruct what was in the pre-read.

4. **No documented outcome.** The meeting ends and three people have three different memories of what was decided.

[Patrick Lencioni's *Death by Meeting*](https://www.tablegroup.com/books/dbm/) is the book on this topic, though the advice is more useful as a taxonomy than a prescription.
