# Film review — Anatomy of a Fall

Justine Triet's courtroom drama begins with a body at the bottom of a house and spends two and a half hours not resolving whether it was a fall, suicide, or murder. The ambiguity is the point — by the end, you've decided, and what you've decided says more about you than the film.

Sandra Hüller is extraordinary. Her character, Sandra, is a novelist whose husband dies under ambiguous circumstances. She becomes the defendant in a French criminal trial. The film's structure is the trial, but its subject is how well we can ever know another person — especially one we've lived with.

The central evidence is a tape recording of an argument between Sandra and her husband. The argument was recorded secretly, and it's devastating — not because it reveals guilt but because it reveals the private violence of a real marriage. Competitiveness, resentment, exhaustion, misunderstood intentions. Everyone watching it in the courtroom (and everyone watching the film) brings their own interpretation.

The blind child who witnessed the accident is the film's most affecting element. His testimony is careful, loving, uncertain, and more honest than any of the adult testimony in the film.

Triet withholds the answer. The film earns this withholding because the trial itself is genuinely constructed to support either conclusion.

Rating: 9/10. Winner of the 2023 Palme d'Or at Cannes. [Mubi's page for the film](https://mubi.com/films/anatomy-of-a-fall) has a good critical overview.
