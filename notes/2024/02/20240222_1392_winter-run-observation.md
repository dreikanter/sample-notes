# Observation — running in winter

The quality of a winter run is hard to explain to people who don't do it. Today: 3°C, clear sky, the park mostly empty except for one other runner and a woman walking two dogs at the far end. The pond still half-frozen, the ice dark and matte in the morning light.

There's a specific kind of consciousness that arrives around kilometer two of a cold run when the body has warmed up and the cold has stopped registering as uncomfortable. It's not euphoria — I'm skeptical of runner's high as commonly described — but something more like the noise in the head getting quiet. The ongoing argument with yourself about things you've said and should have said and will say gets smaller.

The park in winter is emptier and more legible. You can see through the trees to the fence line, see the actual shape of the ground rather than what's growing on it. The pond does something in winter that it doesn't do in summer — it becomes a mirror for the sky and the bare trees, and the reflection is more accurate than the reflections in summer because the surface is more still.

I've been running seriously for two months now (see [[20240216_1372]]) and have started to understand what people mean when they say they need it. It's not about fitness exactly. It's about having one hour per day where the task is simple and the feedback is immediate and the direction is forward.

The crows are always in the same three trees in this park. I have noticed this but don't know what to make of it. Maybe nothing. Maybe they have a reason.

Reference: [What I Talk About When I Talk About Running by Haruki Murakami](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/297657/what-i-talk-about-when-i-talk-about-running-by-haruki-murakami/) — the best book about running I've read, and it isn't really about running.
