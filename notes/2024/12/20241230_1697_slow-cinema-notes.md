# Slow cinema — an introduction to the genre

Spent part of the holiday watching slow cinema films for the first time with any sustained attention. Notes on what the genre is and what I found in it.

## What is slow cinema?

Slow cinema is a loose term for films characterized by long takes, minimal dialogue, contemplative pace, and often an absence of conventional narrative structure. The name is descriptive more than prescriptive. Major directors associated with it: Béla Tarr, Andrei Tarkovsky, Theo Angelopoulos, Chantal Akerman, Carlos Reygadas, Nuri Bilge Ceylan.

## What I watched

**Jeanne Dielman, 23 Commerce Quay, 1080 Brussels** (Akerman, 1975): Three and a half hours following a widow through three days of domestic routine — cooking, cleaning, receiving clients as a sex worker. The film's power comes from its patience: you watch her make dinner so many times that you notice exactly when the routine begins to break down. It's a film about the texture of labour and the violence underneath performed normality. The [Criterion essay on Jeanne Dielman](https://www.criterion.com/current/posts/7878-the-art-of-invisible-labor) is essential.

**Werckmeister Harmonies** (Tarr, 2000): Black and white, 39 shots in the entire film. A strange man in a Hungarian town, a travelling circus with a whale, the feeling of order about to collapse. I don't fully understand it. That doesn't feel like a failure.

## What the form does

Slow cinema resists the default viewing mode — passive, quick-cut, emotionally prompted by score. It puts the viewer in a position of genuine attention. You either meet the film where it is, or you become impatient. Either response is informative.

The long take creates something different from montage: instead of controlling where you look, it lets you choose. That freedom is unsettling and eventually pleasurable.
