# Stained glass — observations from church visit

Stopped into the medieval church while passing through a nearby market town. Had been meaning to visit for a year after seeing a reference in a local architectural survey.

The east window is 15th-century. Not the most famous medieval stained glass in the country — that's at Canterbury, York, or Chartres — but intact and vivid. The reds are the thing: medieval glassmakers achieved a particular saturated red through copper compounds that gives a warmth that modern reproduction glass can't match. In the afternoon light it was almost internal — luminous from within.

The technique: medieval glass was coloured by adding metal oxides to the glass batch (copper for red and green, cobalt for blue, manganese for purple) rather than painting on the surface. This means the colour goes all the way through. The figurative details — faces, drapery folds, architectural details — were added in grisaille (black or brown enamel) painted on the surface and fired. Some pieces are additionally flashed: a very thin coloured layer fused to clear glass, allowing the glassmaker to scratch back through to clear for details.

The structural lead lines (cames) aren't just practical — a skilled designer uses them compositionally, as drawing lines that define and divide the composition.

The [Victoria and Albert Museum's stained glass collection guide](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/stained-glass-techniques) has a good overview of the techniques.

I spent 40 minutes in a church I hadn't planned to stop at, which is about the best kind of detour there is.
