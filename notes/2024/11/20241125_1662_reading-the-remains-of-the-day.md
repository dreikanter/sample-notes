# Reading notes — The Remains of the Day

Kazuo Ishiguro, 1989. Read over about ten days — not because it's difficult but because it deserves slow reading.

Stevens is one of the great unreliable narrators in English fiction, but his unreliability is structural rather than malicious. He genuinely doesn't see what the reader sees — or rather, he sees it and then doesn't quite let himself see it. The accumulation of these partial-avoidances is the novel.

The professional pride subplot — Stevens' obsession with what constitutes "dignity" in a butler — is not just a metaphor for his emotional repression but is doing something more interesting: Ishiguro is looking at how the vocabulary of professionalism becomes a tool for not having to confront certain things. "I was doing my job" as the answer to every question that would otherwise be uncomfortable.

Miss Kenton's letters are used brilliantly. They function as a kind of second narrator — not unreliable in the same way, but filtered through Stevens' reading of them, which introduces its own distortions.

The England-in-decline backdrop (the house changing hands, the American owner, the receding postwar world) works as social context without being allegorical in a heavy-handed way. It's a novel about people in a particular historical moment, not a statement about that moment.

The ending is quietly devastating in a way that creeps up on you. The bench scene. The brief admission. The decision to look at the remains of the day.

Worth reading alongside [Ishiguro's Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/literature/2017/ishiguro/lecture/) where he talks about his concerns as a novelist — some of it retroactively illuminates what he was doing with Stevens.
