# On walking as thinking

There's a genre of writing about walking that tends toward the mystical — Rousseau's reveries, Thoreau's sauntering, Wordsworth composing poems while pacing the terraces at Dove Cottage. I've always been skeptical of the grandiosity but less skeptical of the underlying claim: that walking does something to thinking that sitting doesn't.

The research exists and is fairly consistent. A [Stanford study on creativity and walking](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2014/04/walking-vs-sitting-042414) found that creative output increased by roughly 60% when people walked versus sat. The effect held even on a treadmill facing a blank wall. It's not the scenery; it's the movement.

My own experience maps onto this unevenly. I don't have revelatory insights while walking. What happens instead is that problems become less urgent — I can hold them more loosely. The physical engagement of navigating terrain (even familiar terrain) takes up just enough attentional bandwidth that the background process running on a problem isn't being constantly interrupted by the urge to *do something* about it.

There's also something about the pace. Walking is slow enough that you encounter the same things repeatedly — the same corner, the same tree, the same view — which creates a kind of forced revisiting. Ideas come back in different lights.

I've started taking a 30-minute walk before the hardest task of the day rather than coffee. Results: less anxiety, more clarity on the first thing I do when I sit down. This is preliminary and anecdotal. But it's become a thing I do.
