# Risotto technique notes

After making risotto maybe forty times and still getting it wrong occasionally, I'm writing down what actually works.

## The fundamentals

**Rice**: Carnaroli is worth seeking out over Arborio — higher starch content, holds its shape better, gives a creamier result. [Serious Eats' risotto breakdown](https://www.seriouseats.com/the-best-risotto) is worth reading.

**Stock**: must be hot. Adding cold stock drops the temperature of the rice and breaks the even cooking. Keep it at a simmer in a separate pan throughout.

**The soffritto**: Low and slow, don't rush it. The onion should be completely translucent and sweet before the rice goes in — 8–10 minutes minimum.

**Toasting the rice**: Add to the pan, stir in the fat, and cook for 2–3 minutes until the edges of each grain turn slightly translucent. Don't skip this.

**Wine**: Add a glass of dry white wine after toasting, stir until completely absorbed. The alcohol cooks off; the acidity remains and brightens the final dish.

**The stock additions**: Ladle by ladle, each absorbed before the next. The conventional wisdom is to stir constantly — I think this is overclaimed. Frequent stirring is enough; constant stirring is tiring and doesn't significantly improve the result.

**Mantecatura**: This is the key step that home cooks often skip. Take the pan off the heat. Add cold butter cut into cubes and Parmesan, then beat aggressively — the emulsion is what makes restaurant risotto glossy and flowing rather than stiff and gluey. Let it rest 2 minutes, then serve immediately.

**Consistency check**: The risotto should flow when you shake the pan — "all'onda" (in waves). If it mounds, it's too stiff and too dry.

Serves 4 in about 30 minutes once the soffritto is done.
