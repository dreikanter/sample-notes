# On slow travel — a reflection

I've been thinking about the difference between visiting a place and spending time in one, prompted by planning the Portugal trip [[20241119_1656]].

The conventional tourist itinerary is an optimisation problem: maximum sites per day, hit the highlights, come home with evidence of having been somewhere. I've done this and found it exhausting and, on reflection, only faintly satisfying. You've collected data rather than experienced anything.

The alternative — spending longer in fewer places, repeating some of the same routes, eating at the same café twice — feels initially like missing out, but delivers something different. You start to notice how the light changes through the day. You overhear conversations, follow people's faces, understand something about the rhythm of a place. This is not deep cultural knowledge; let's not be grandiose. But it's actual experience rather than documentation of having been.

The writer Rolf Potts wrote a book called *Vagabonding* that [makes this argument at length](https://www.vagabonding.net) — that slowness isn't just a style of travel but a different relationship to time and attention. What I find more interesting than his framework is the underlying observation that almost everything we want from travel is incompatible with speed.

For the Portugal trip, I'm leaning toward Porto for five nights, one day trip, no more than four specific plans. The rest improvised. This is mildly anxiety-inducing — the fear of "wasting" time — and I suspect working through that anxiety is part of the point.
