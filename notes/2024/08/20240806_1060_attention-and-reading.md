# Attention and reading — notes on "The Shallows"

Nicholas Carr's central claim in *The Shallows* is that the internet is not just a tool we use but an environment we inhabit, and that environment shapes the neural pathways involved in reading and attention. The argument draws on neuroplasticity research — specifically the idea that the brain physically rewires itself in response to repeated patterns of use.

The part I found most convincing: the distinction between what Carr calls "deep reading" and the skimming, hyperlink-following mode that web reading encourages. Deep reading, he argues, is not a natural state — it had to be trained into us by the technology of the codex book, and it can be untrained. He cites his own experience of losing the ability to read long texts without his attention fragmenting.

The part I found least convincing: the implication that previous information technologies (printing press, clock) caused comparably disruptive cognitive shifts but somehow stabilised without catastrophic loss. He doesn't show why this technology would be different in kind rather than degree.

What I keep thinking about: his discussion of Nietzsche acquiring a typewriter and the documented change in his prose style — shorter, more aphoristic — as a possible early example of medium shaping thought. Whether that's a loss or not depends entirely on what you value.

Practically, I've started keeping my phone out of the room when reading. Anecdotally, the difference is noticeable within a few days.

Related reading: [Maryanne Wolf on reading and the reading brain](https://www.maryannewolf.com/reader-come-home-1) — *Reader, Come Home* covers similar ground with more neuroscience detail.
