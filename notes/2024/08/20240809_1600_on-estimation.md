# On estimation in software projects

The rate-limiting project took three weeks of implementation. Original estimate: ten days. This is not an unusual ratio.

After many cycles of this, some honest observations.

## Why estimates are usually wrong

**We estimate the known work.** The estimate covers the parts we understand. It doesn't cover the integration problem we'll discover on day 4, the library quirk that requires a workaround, the review feedback that requires a rethink of the approach. These unknowns take time but can't be estimated because they're not yet known.

**We estimate the person who knows the problem now.** After three days on a problem, you understand it well enough to do it quickly. The estimate is made before those three days.

**We conflate optimism about the future with probability.** "If everything goes smoothly, this takes a week." This is true. But a world where everything goes smoothly is not the world we consistently inhabit.

## What actually helps

**Reference class forecasting (from Kahneman [[20241007_1589]]):** How long have similar projects actually taken? Not "seems like similar" — actually look up past projects and their actuals. Use that distribution.

**Separate estimate from commitment.** The estimate is your best prediction. The commitment is a different conversation involving risk tolerance, buffer, and priorities. Conflating them causes people to estimate defensively.

**Track actuals vs estimates explicitly.** You'll never improve without feedback. The data is humbling but corrective.

The [Cone of Uncertainty](https://www.stevemcconnell.com/blog/) in software is real: early in a project, estimates have wide error bars. Commit late on estimates, early on directions.
