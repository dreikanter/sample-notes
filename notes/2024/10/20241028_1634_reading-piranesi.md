# Reading notes — Piranesi

Susanna Clarke, 2020. Finished in two sittings over a weekend.

The setup is brilliant and the restraint in holding the central mystery is almost perfectly calibrated. Piranesi — we learn to call him that, though that's not his name — inhabits a vast house of halls, statues, tides, and moons, and he has accepted this world so completely that his journals read as a kind of ecstatic natural history. He catalogs the tides, studies the statues, counts the dead he has found. The strangeness of his situation is made strange again through his contentment with it.

Clarke resists the obvious move of framing this as a tragedy waiting to happen. The revelation of what the house is and what has been done to Piranesi arrives sideways — through his own slowly reconstructed understanding — and the emotional register of that process is something I haven't encountered done quite this way before.

The Piranesi of the title is a reference to Giovanni Battista Piranesi, the 18th-century Italian artist whose imaginary prison etchings (*Carceri d'invenzione*) are exactly as dreamlike and architecturally impossible as the house in the novel. The [Piranesi etchings at the Metropolitan Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/388492) are worth looking at alongside the book.

The book is short enough to read fast and rich enough to reward reading slowly. I did both. First sitting: tearing through it. Second: rereading the journal entries more carefully, noticing what he's not able to see.

A novel about memory and identity that is also, genuinely, a strange and specific kind of love story.
