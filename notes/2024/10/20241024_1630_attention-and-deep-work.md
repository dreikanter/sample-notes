# Attention and deep work — some notes

I've been working through Cal Newport's *Deep Work* again, more critically this time. The core argument holds: the ability to focus without distraction on cognitively demanding tasks is increasingly rare and increasingly valuable. That's probably correct.

Where I think he strays is in the implicit assumption that depth and breadth are always in tension. Some of the most generative work I've done came from promiscuous reading across fields — staying with something long enough to connect it to something else. That's not the same as the constant shallow attention he critiques, but it looks similar from the outside.

More useful to me than Newport's framework has been David Cain's writing on [Raptitude](https://www.raptitude.com/2016/12/how-to-carry-out-a-plan/) about the gap between intention and execution. The planning problem isn't usually knowing *what* to do — it's bridging the moment of transition from not-doing to doing. That first two minutes is where most intended deep work dies.

My current approach: time-block mornings (9–12) as protected for the hardest thing on the board. Phone in another room. No email before noon. This isn't novel. The part that actually required adjustment was not scheduling meetings before 1pm — which required some negotiation with colleagues but has been worth it.

The thing Newport doesn't address enough is recovery. Deep focus depletes something real. Without deliberate rest — actual rest, not passive scrolling — the capacity erodes over the week. I've been more intentional about 20-minute walks between sessions and it makes a measurable difference to afternoon quality.
