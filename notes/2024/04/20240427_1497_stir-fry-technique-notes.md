# Stir-fry technique notes

Home stir-fry has a ceiling that restaurant stir-fry doesn't. The reason is wok hei — the characteristic breath-of-the-wok charred quality that comes from the very high heat of commercial burners (130,000 BTU versus a home burner's 15,000-25,000). You can approximate it but not replicate it.

That said, home stir-fry can be excellent if you respect the physics.

## What actually matters

**Dry ingredients:** Any moisture on vegetables or meat will steam rather than sear. Pat protein dry with paper towel. Dry vegetables after washing. This is the most important rule.

**Hot pan before adding oil:** Heat until smoking. Then add oil. This is backward from Western cooking practice.

**Small batches:** A home burner cannot maintain high heat if you crowd the wok. Cook in batches smaller than you think. For a two-serving meal, two or three separate additions to the wok are normal.

**Mise en place is mandatory:** Stir-fry moves in 2-3 minutes. Everything must be prepped before the burner goes on.

**The sauce goes in last:** Add sauce at the end, toss to coat, serve immediately. Sauce that sits in the wok thickens from reduction and loses brightness.

## On velveting

Coating protein in cornstarch + egg white + salt (and sometimes baking soda) before cooking. Changes the texture of the meat from chewy to silky. Worth doing for chicken and beef, essential for pork. Takes 30 minutes for the coating to set.

## What I've improved recently

Using a much higher oil-to-vegetables ratio than I was — restaurant stir-fry uses more oil than looks reasonable from a health standpoint. The oil is what carries heat to all surfaces.
