# On estimation

Software estimation is famously unreliable, and the usual framing is that programmers are bad at it. The more interesting question is why, and the answer isn't "programmers are overoptimistic" (though they often are) — it's that estimation is inherently an exercise in imagining a future you haven't built yet, and the things that take the longest are by definition the things you hadn't anticipated.

The tasks that take their estimated time are the ones you've done before in a form similar enough to recognize. The tasks that balloon are the ones where something unexpected sits behind what looked like a door: a library that behaves differently than documented, a data quality problem you didn't know existed, an architectural constraint that requires revisiting decisions already made.

A useful heuristic I've started using: estimate the work you can see, then ask what class of thing might be hiding. Not trying to enumerate the hidden things (that's impossible) but to ask: is there a dependency I haven't verified? Is there data I'm assuming is clean? Is there a system I don't fully understand?

Even without identifying the specific surprises, this question changes the estimate. If the answer is "yes, there's a system I don't fully understand," adding 50% is probably conservative.

The second useful adjustment: estimate in units larger than you feel comfortable with. Estimates given in hours tend to assume optimal conditions; estimates given in days naturally incorporate some buffering for reality. The granularity of the estimate signals its precision.

None of this makes estimation good. It makes it slightly less systematically wrong.
