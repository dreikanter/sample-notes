# Reading notes — The rings of Saturn

W.G. Sebald, 1995, translated by Michael Hulse 1998. Got to it finally (see [[20240401_1462]]). 

The book describes a walking journey through Suffolk, England. But that's not really what it is — the walk is an occasion for Sebald to range through time and association: the herring fisheries of the North Sea, the Ottoman court, Joseph Conrad's Congo journey, Roger Casement, Rembrandt's *The Anatomy Lesson*, the silk trade, the devastation of the landscape. These connect loosely, thematically rather than causally, linked by the associative logic of a melancholy mind moving through a landscape saturated with history and loss.

The prose (in translation) is famous: very long sentences, subordinate clauses accumulating like geological strata. There's a quality of someone speaking aloud while walking — the thought unfolds as it moves. The black-and-white photographs embedded in the text (as in all his work) are slightly off — either reproduced slightly blurrily or chosen to avoid clarity. They suggest evidence without offering it.

What Sebald is doing with time: the present walk and the historical material exist in the same temporal zone. Events from the 1840s are described with the same immediacy as what the narrator sees on the path today. The effect is that history becomes present and haunting rather than safely past.

I read 80 pages and had to stop not because it's not absorbing but because the sustained melancholy is genuinely heavy. This is a book to read in smaller portions.

The blurring of fact and fiction is deliberately maintained: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2019/aug/17/wg-sebald-the-rings-of-saturn-anniversary
