# On attention

Simone Weil wrote that "attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." The context was religious — paying full attention to a suffering person as an act of love — but the observation points to something more general about attention as a finite and valuable resource.

The contemporary concern about attention is usually framed as distraction: phones, notifications, the endless scroll. This framing isn't wrong but it focuses on the mechanism of loss rather than the nature of what's being lost. What is sustained attention actually like, and what does it enable?

My rough experience: the good work — in any domain — happens after sustained attention has been held for long enough that the obvious approaches have been exhausted and something less obvious emerges. This takes longer than fifteen minutes. It's disrupted by interruptions that don't just take the time of the interruption but reset the concentration state.

The question isn't whether smartphones are bad (too binary, too moral) but what kind of work requires what kind of attention, and whether you can choose the conditions for that work. Some work is genuinely compatible with distraction — the diffuse attention state can even be useful for certain kinds of synthesis. Deep original thinking seems not to be.

There's a species of productivity advice that tries to engineer around this: time-blocking, phone-free zones, structured deep work periods. These help but they treat the symptom. The harder question is motivational — do I actually want to do the difficult sustained work, or do I prefer the feeling of doing many easier things and calling it a productive day?
