# Reflections on place

The conversation with Marguerite about the orchard (see [[20240323_1446]]) has stayed with me. The idea that a place carries its history invisibly — that you can walk past something thousands of times without knowing what it was — points to a question about what it means to know a place.

There's a difference between familiarity and knowledge. I'm familiar with this neighborhood in the sense of automatic navigation: I know which intersection has the broken light, which dog will bark from which yard. This familiarity is a kind of competence, not a kind of understanding.

The deeper knowledge — what the orchard was, why this street was laid out this way, who built that particular house and what they were trying for — requires either time and attention or access to people who have it. Marguerite's thirty years here bought her the stories. Most people move more frequently and don't get them.

Robert Macfarlane writes about what he calls "the entangled depth of landscape" — the way places hold ecological and historical time simultaneously in their surfaces. The idea is more vivid in Suffolk or the Scottish highlands than in a suburban neighborhood, but it's not absent here. The slight rise where the farm stand was. The remaining old trees that the developers worked around. The lot that's been vacant for thirty years.

Looking at places as dense with meaning rather than empty backdrop requires a different kind of attention than I usually bring — slower, more question-forming.
