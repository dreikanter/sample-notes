# Book notes — Bullshit jobs

David Graeber, 2018. Read the original 2013 Strike! Magazine essay and the book expansion. The essay is sharper and more polemical; the book adds empirical texture but is longer than it needs to be.

The core claim: a significant portion of modern employment consists of jobs that the people performing them believe provide no social value. Graeber distinguishes:

- **Bullshit jobs:** Jobs the worker knows are pointless — corporate lawyers who know the work makes nothing better, consultants who write reports no one reads, managers who exist to manage managers
- **Shit jobs:** Jobs that are genuinely necessary but underpaid and undervalued — sanitation, nursing, food production

The paradox: the more clearly useful a job is, the less it tends to pay. The inverse also holds.

The book is strongest on the phenomenology of bullshit work — the testimonies from people describing what their work actually consists of, the psychological damage of being paid well to do something you believe is meaningless. The existential dimension is real and underexplored in labor literature.

Where it's weaker: the proposed mechanisms (managerial feudalism, the moral framework that ties work to social worth) are suggestive but not rigorously argued. Graeber was an anthropologist, not an economist, and the structural claims rest more on plausibility than evidence.

Still: the original question is genuinely interesting and the book takes it seriously. The identification of "bullshit" as a distinct category rather than just inefficiency is a useful analytic move.

The original essay for comparison: https://www.strike.coop/bullshit-jobs/
