# On expertise

The interesting thing about becoming competent at something is how it changes what you notice. A novice and an expert can look at the same thing and see fundamentally different objects. The novice sees the surface; the expert sees the structure that generates the surface, the decisions that were made, the alternatives that weren't chosen.

This is satisfying as expertise grows and disorienting when you enter a new domain. I've been learning to identify plants seriously for about a year — not a botanical level of expertise, more a serious amateur — and I've started to see the landscape differently. Where I saw "plants" before, I now see specific species with histories and ecologies. The trout lily patch has a different reality to me now than it did in March. But going to the concert last week, I was aware of how much I wasn't hearing: the structural relationships in the Beethoven that someone with music theory training would have as foreground and I had as background.

The intermediate state is uncomfortable. You know enough to know what you don't know — the Dunning-Kruger curve's long middle slope. The novice has confidence born of ignorance; the expert has confidence built from deep knowledge; the intermediate practitioner has the anxiety of partial knowledge.

Metacognition about learning seems to genuinely help: knowing that the uncomfortable middle exists makes it feel less like failure.
