# Reading notes — Babel

R.F. Kuang, 2022. Full title: *Babel, or The Necessity of Violence: An Arcane History of the Oxford Translators' Revolution*.

The premise: a version of Victorian England where silver bars engraved with words in two languages generate power from the semantic difference between them — a technology that underlies British imperial power. The protagonists are students from colonized nations recruited to Oxford's Royal Institute of Translation (Babel) to produce and maintain this system, while increasingly aware that their labor and their languages are being extracted.

Kuang is explicit about her thesis in a way that literary fiction usually isn't. The novel is an argument about colonialism, extraction, and the complicity of those who serve powerful systems in exchange for partial inclusion. The characters discuss this directly. This makes the book less subtle than it might have been and more useful as a political document.

The Oxford setting is rendered in enough detail that it feels researched without being nostalgic about it. The critique of the university as institution — the library as repository of stolen goods, the quadrangles maintained by invisible labor — is embedded in the physical descriptions.

Where it gets complicated: the "necessity of violence" in the subtitle is addressed directly in the final third. The book asks whether reform from inside is possible or whether only rupture changes systems fundamentally. It doesn't find a comfortable answer.

Long and somewhat overpopulated with characters, but the central four are well-drawn.
