# On deadlines

A project deadline was moved up by two weeks yesterday. The initial response — mild panic, recalculation, the sensation of the horizon moving closer — is familiar. What's also familiar is that the work usually gets done anyway, often at roughly the same quality, because deadlines are focusing rather than compressing.

The standard explanation: Parkinson's Law, work expands to fill time. There's something to this. I've watched myself spend a week on a problem that I solved in two days when the timeline tightened. Some of that week was productive — better solution, cleaner code — and some of it was refinement that went past diminishing returns.

What the deadline actually does: forces explicit prioritization. When everything has to fit in two weeks instead of four, you can no longer defer the question of what matters enough to keep. The things that get cut are usually the things that were lower priority than I was acknowledging.

The danger on the other side: deadlines that are too tight produce work that is genuinely incomplete rather than strategically scoped. The difference matters but isn't always visible to the person setting the deadline. The negotiation — I can deliver X by the date, or X+Y if you extend — requires trust that the person on the delivery side isn't just asking for more time to do the same work.

I think I'm generally faster than my initial estimates when I'm focused on the work rather than thinking about it from a distance. The estimate expands too, following its own version of Parkinson's Law.
