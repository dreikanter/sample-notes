# On walking to think

The argument for walking as thinking aid is both obvious and slightly mysterious. The obvious part: movement disrupts the stuck states that come from sitting with a problem, the rhythmic bilateral movement seems to facilitate certain kinds of associative thinking, and getting away from the screen removes the compulsion to look something up rather than think it through.

The mysterious part: which problems benefit from walking and which don't. My rough experience is that walking is good for problems that need synthesis — connecting things you already know in a new pattern — and bad for problems that require holding a lot of specifics in working memory simultaneously. If I need to design a database schema, walking doesn't help. If I need to figure out how to approach a difficult conversation, it often does.

There's also a question of whether the good thinking is actually happening during the walk or whether the walk is just clearing space for the thinking to happen afterward. Nietzsche said "Only thoughts which come from walking have any value," which is good rhetoric but probably overclaims the causation.

What I notice practically: the best walking-thoughts are ones I almost immediately forget unless I stop to write them down. There's something about the state of walking that doesn't transfer well to stationary memory. I've started carrying a small notebook and the friction of stopping and writing is genuinely useful — it forces a quick test of whether the thought is actually good or just felt good while moving.
