# Spring observation — first week of March

The light shifted this week in a way that I notice every year and am always slightly surprised by. Not the daily incremental return of the sun — I track that — but a qualitative shift where the angle of light produces warmth rather than just brightness. On Saturday at 2pm the south-facing wall of the apartment was genuinely warm to the touch.

The garden is doing what it does: the garlic is six inches tall and the spacing I chose is starting to resolve into individual plants rather than an undifferentiated green wash. The peas I planted a week early (see [[20240304_1394]]) have germinated — eight out of twelve cells showing, which is good enough. The frost cloth held through the low of -3°C on Tuesday.

The tomato seedlings are two weeks old and at the "hope" stage: small, pale, dependent on the grow lights, doing nothing visible but quietly putting on root mass. By April they'll be recognizable as tomato plants.

The cold frame is contributing spinach. Not abundantly — maybe a large handful every five days — but it's growing. First time I've eaten something from the garden in winter. The spinach is sweeter and more tender than summer spinach; the cold does something to the flavor.

Spring operates on the gardener's psychology before it operates on the garden. There's a specific ambition that arrives with the light, a sense that this year you'll catch up on everything you deferred, plant more, miss nothing. I know this feeling well enough to be skeptical of it and still feel it.

Something from [[1012]] about seasonal attention seems relevant here — the idea of working with natural rhythms rather than against them.

Reference: [Eliot Coleman on spring soil](https://www.chelseagreen.com/product/four-season-harvest/) — the patience required before working wet soil.
