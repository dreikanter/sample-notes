# On rereading

Picked up a novel I first read in my early twenties — won't say which one because describing what you loved about a book you loved at twenty-two is dangerous territory. The edition is the same one, paperback spine cracked in the specific way that means it was carried in a bag for a while.

The experience of rereading is strange. You can't un-know what happens, which removes suspense entirely, but it opens a different kind of attention — you notice how the author signals the ending from the first chapter, the economy of certain passages you skipped over on first reading, the moments that felt obvious and now feel precise.

What surprised me: I had misremembered large portions. A character I remembered as central is actually quite minor. A scene I could have described in detail didn't happen the way I thought. My memory had edited the book into the book I needed at twenty-two, which is a different object than the one sitting on the table.

This is probably normal. We encode texts through the filter of who we are when we read them. The remembered version and the actual text diverge with time. Rereading collapses the two and creates a third thing that is neither the original experience nor the plain present-tense text.

Patricia Meyer Spacks wrote an entire book about this: *On Rereading* (2011). I haven't read it but the thesis — that rereading tells us about ourselves more than about books — seems plausible from this evening's data point.
