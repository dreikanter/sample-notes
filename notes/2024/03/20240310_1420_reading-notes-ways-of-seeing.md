# Reading notes — Ways of seeing

John Berger, 1972. Slim but relentless. Based on the BBC television series of the same name. I came to it because someone quoted the opening line — "Seeing comes before words" — in a talk about interface design, which is a bit of a stretch but not wrong.

The core argument: the way we see images is shaped by everything we know, assume, and believe before we look. Berger wants to strip away the mystification that the art establishment uses to separate "legitimate" interpretation from ordinary response. He's particularly sharp about oil painting as a medium that emerged simultaneously with capitalism and how it depicts accumulated property — fields, animals, silver, cloth, wives — as stable and permanent, a promise that what you own stays yours.

The chapter on the female nude is the one most people cite. The argument that the nude (female body as object for the male gaze) differs fundamentally from the naked (a person aware of being seen) still holds up as a distinction. He uses Rubens vs. Rembrandt's late self-portraits to show the difference.

Chapter 7 is entirely images with no text — a survey of advertising photographs. The juxtaposition with Old Master paintings makes the point without commentary. Advertising promises you the social presence that the painting promised the merchant class, except now it's democratized and therefore hollow.

Weaknesses: Berger overreaches on the universality of the "male gaze" as the only organizing principle. He doesn't account for women looking at female nudes, or for the complexity of desire. But 1972 means some slack is due.
