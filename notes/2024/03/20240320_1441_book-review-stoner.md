# Book review — Stoner

John Williams, 1965. 278 pages. This book has had an unusual afterlife — out of print for years, then quietly championed by literary readers, then a bestseller in Europe before American publishers noticed.

The plot summary sounds catastrophically depressing: William Stoner, a farmer's son who becomes an English professor, has a failed marriage, a thwarted love affair, departmental feuds, and achieves nothing that the world considers significant. He reads the sentence on a student's paper that changes his life — Sonnet 73, "That time of year thou mayst in me behold" — and devotes himself to literature.

What Williams actually delivers is not a tragedy but something stranger: a quiet argument that the examined life, even the externally defeated one, has a quality of fullness that conventional success misses. Stoner's love for literature is not a compensation for failure; it is the thing that is real.

The prose is clean and precise and never congratulates itself. The sentences do exactly the work required and stop. The cruelty of Lomax (Stoner's departmental enemy) is rendered without melodrama — bureaucratic, petty, persistent, recognizable. The affair with Katherine Driscoll is the warmest section of the book and Williams is careful not to sentimentalize it.

The ending is worth reading twice. A man takes stock of a life in a few pages and the reader is left to decide what kind of life it was.

Verdict: essential. One of the novels I'll probably reread — see [[20240314_1428]].
