# Reading notes — The Real World of Technology, Ursula Franklin

Reading this as research for the long-form essay [[20240830_1568]]. Published 1990 based on a CBC Massey Lectures series. Still remarkably current.

## The holistic/prescriptive distinction

Franklin's central distinction is between technologies that allow the practitioner to respond to changing conditions (holistic) versus those that break work into sequences of prescribed steps that can be supervised and standardized (prescriptive).

Pottery is holistic — the potter responds continuously to the clay, the kiln's temperature, the moisture. An assembly line is prescriptive — each worker does a defined step regardless of what's happening elsewhere.

The argument: modern societies have extended prescriptive technologies far beyond manufacturing into domains — education, governance, care work — where holistic response is actually essential. We've applied production templates to practice.

## What resonated most

Chapter 4 on "the real costs of technology." Franklin argues that we systematically undercount costs by assigning them to third parties (environmental costs, social disruption costs, health costs) who don't participate in the technology's benefits. The calculation that makes a technology "efficient" only works if you ignore externalities.

This is not a new observation but she makes it with great precision.

## The relevance to my essay

The holistic/prescriptive distinction maps cleanly onto software design. Most software is profoundly prescriptive — it defines valid inputs, acceptable behaviors, correct sequences. The question of who designed those prescriptions and whose needs they reflect is exactly what my essay is about.
