# On keeping records — what gets lost and what doesn't

I went back through notes from three years ago looking for something specific and found myself reading for forty minutes instead. The notes I had taken were detailed in odd ways — specific sensory information, exact quotes, weather — but almost entirely absent of anything about my emotional state at the time, which is exactly what I was curious about.

This is a known problem with how I take notes. I record the external and the factual because those feel documentable. I don't record the internal because it feels either too obvious (I'll remember how I felt, surely) or too private (which is strange given no one else reads these).

Three years later, I don't remember how I felt. The specific sensory information — the weather, the quote from whoever I was with — retrieves the scene but not the state. The scene is a room I can see into but not enter.

There's a question of what notes are actually for. The dominant productivity-adjacent framing says notes are an external memory system, inputs to a second brain or knowledge graph. That model has some uses but it doesn't account for the retrospective dimension — using notes not to retrieve information but to reconstruct experience.

Rolf Dobelli wrote dismissively about diary-keeping as a form of self-indulgence. I think he's wrong but I find it harder to articulate why than I'd like. Maybe because the value is not in the product but in the practice — the discipline of stopping and attending to what is actually happening, which is something the current moment always resists.

I've started adding one sentence at the end of each note that tries to describe the mood of the writing session rather than its content.
