# On finishing things

A friend mentioned offhand that she has trouble finishing things — not starting them, not sustaining them mid-arc, but the last ten percent. I've been thinking about that since because it's precisely my pattern too, and I haven't found a clean explanation for it.

The start of something is obvious: high novelty, high uncertainty resolved quickly into forward motion, the pleasurable feeling of a thing coming into existence. The middle is harder but it has its own momentum, a channel you've dug that water runs through. The end is different. The end requires a decision about what it is. When something is finished, it exists outside you and can be evaluated, found wanting, compared. While it's unfinished it still holds all its potential.

I've read this described as perfectionism, and sometimes it probably is. But perfectionism as an explanation doesn't quite account for the cases where the unfinished thing is clearly better than nothing and you know it. There's something else operating — something more like a reluctance to let go of the relationship with the thing while it's still becoming.

The practical workaround I've settled on is arbitrary deadlines with low stakes. If something has to be done by Thursday for no reason except that Thursday is a real day, it gets done. The fiction of the deadline substitutes for the internal pressure of caring about the outcome.

Paul Graham's old essay [Holding a Program in Your Head](http://paulgraham.com/head.html) is tangentially relevant — his argument about the cost of interruptions maps onto why half-finished projects decay: the mental representation degrades and re-entry gets harder.
